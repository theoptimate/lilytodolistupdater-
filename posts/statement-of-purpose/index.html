A statement of purpose

What this site is for, what it will refuse to do, who pays for it, and what is already wrong with it.

Data to the People 29 August 2026 Reading time · 4 min

Almost everything a government does is already public. Proclamations go into the Federal Register the week they are signed. Agencies publish their own performance data, usually reluctantly and usually as a spreadsheet nobody opens. Disaster declarations, roll calls, recall notices, grant obligations — all of it is out there, technically available to anyone who wants it.

Almost none of it is legible. The record is open and the picture is closed, and the distance between those two things is not a technical problem. It is a labour problem. Somebody has to sit down with the primary source, decide what counts as one of the thing being counted, do the counting, and then draw it. That work is unglamorous, it does not scale, and it is where this site lives.

Three rules

A graphic goes up here when three things are true. They are not aspirations; they are the conditions.

It comes from the primary record. Not from an article about the record. Not from a press release summarising the record. Most wrong numbers on the internet are wrong because somebody copied a number from somebody who copied it from somebody, and the original had a footnote that got lost on the second hop. If the primary source cannot be reached, the graphic says so on its face rather than quietly using the next best thing.

The counting rule is written down before the chart is drawn. This is the part nobody shows you, and it is where almost all the disagreement actually lives. What is one unit? What happens when two of them overlap? What is excluded, and does excluding it change the answer? A chart that will not tell you its counting rule is asking you to trust a decision it has hidden. We would rather you disagree with us precisely than believe us vaguely.

The data ships with the graphic. Every number on a page here — the totals, the shares, the rankings, the big figure at the top — is computed from a dataset inside that page. Nothing is typed in by hand, which means the chart and the caption cannot drift apart after an edit, and it means you can find our mistakes without asking permission. Correct one entry and the whole graphic changes.

The first graphic is not finished

Which brings us to the awkward part, and the reason this post exists on day one rather than a year in.

The graphic this site launches with counts the days the US flag flew at half-staff between 2019 and 2025. Nine of its periods have end dates we inferred from standard practice rather than confirmed against the proclamation itself. They are marked in the data and flagged in the tooltip. It is a working draft, and it says so.

We could have quietly shipped it and hoped. A site whose entire pitch is method rigour, opening with a graphic carrying nine unverified entries, is not a great look. But the alternative — presenting inferred dates as confirmed ones because the chart looks better that way — is precisely the failure this site exists to be an alternative to. If the first post tells you the standard and the first graphic quietly breaks it, the standard was never real.

So: it is a draft, it is labelled a draft, and it gets verified. That is what the labels are for.

Corrections are the practice, not the apology

A correction here is not an embarrassment to be minimised. It is the mechanism working. Send the entry, what it should say, and the source that shows it. The dataset gets fixed, every figure on the page recomputes, the change is logged where readers can see it, and if it moves the headline finding it goes at the top of the graphic rather than into a footnote at the bottom.

Reporters of substantive corrections get credited unless they would rather not be. There is no version of this where finding our error makes you the problem.

Who pays, and what that buys

Advertising. That is the whole model right now, and you should know it before you read anything else here.

Ad-funded publishing pulls in a specific direction: toward volume, toward whatever travels, toward the version of a story that gets clicked rather than the version that is true. Naming the pull is not the same as resisting it, so here is the counterweight, stated so you can hold us to it: the three rules above decide what runs. A graphic that fails them does not get published, however well it would have done.

Practically, that means ad slots are labelled, kept away from navigation and chart controls, and limited to two on an article. Advertisers see nothing before publication and have no involvement in what gets covered. If the advertising ever starts shaping the editorial, the honest thing is to say so on this page rather than let you find out.

What this site will not do

  • Charts built from other charts. If we cannot re-derive it, we do not publish it.
  • Statistical inference. These are counts of a record. A count of a record is a count of what got recorded, which is not always what happened, and we will keep saying so rather than dressing a count up as a finding.
  • Interesting numbers that rest on definitions we cannot defend. Some good stories die at the counting-rule stage. They should.
  • Engagement bait. No outrage framing on a chart, no headline the data does not support, no number stripped of the caveat that makes it honest.

Take it and use it

Everything here is licensed CC BY 4.0. Republish it, adapt it, teach with it, put it in something you sell — credit us and link back. The underlying federal records are public domain and carry no conditions from us at all.

This is not generosity. Work that cannot travel does not do anything. A chart that ends up in a lesson plan, a local paper, or a Wikipedia article has done more than one that stayed here collecting our own pageviews.

The invitation

If you know a public record that deserves this treatment — something published, countable, and currently illegible — tell us. If you find an error, tell us faster. Both go to the same place: hello@datatothepeople.org.

The record is public. Making it legible is the job.

— Data to the People
